Starting a sourdough starter, from the research up

First tests

The three starters

Three different routes, chosen so that each test teaches something: the fastest flour, a method that avoids the false rise, and the blend most bakers keep. All three use small amounts, so there is little waste, and feeds fall at 07:00 and 19:00 to fit a working day.

Method

How to run the test

The three starters only tell us something if they are treated the same way. Start them on the same evening and keep everything else equal.

Set-up

  • Three identical straight-sided glass jars, labelled A, B and C, each weighed empty so you can weigh what is inside.
  • A rubber band on each jar to mark the level right after feeding.
  • The same water for all three. Liestal tap water is fine; let it stand if it smells of chlorine.
  • One warm spot at 24 to 26 °C, with a thermometer next to the jars. In a Swiss winter kitchen (often 19 to 21 °C), the oven with only the light on works, but check it: it can climb past 30 °C. A cool box with a jar of warm water is a gentler option.
  • Flour from one bag per type for the whole test.

What to note at every feed

  • Time and room temperature.
  • Ratio fed (for example 1:2:2) and the flour used.
  • Peak rise as a percentage: (peak height − starting height) ÷ starting height. Doubling is 100%.
  • Hours from feed to peak.
  • Smell (yoghurt, fruity, vinegar, nail polish, cheese) and look (bubbles on top and sides, liquid on top).
  • A photo at the peak.

When a starter passes its ready test, bake the same country loaf with it. A, B and C side by side is the real result.

The short version

What the research agrees on

Where the studies and the bakers line up. Links go to the sources further down this page.

It takes one to two weeks

A new starter goes through a relay of microbes. Bacteria from the flour come first, lactic acid bacteria take over within a few days, and yeast follows once the mix is acidic. Most starters can raise bread after 7 to 14 days and keep improving for a few weeks more. Boreczek 2020, Lau 2021, Wink 2008.

The day-2 rise is a false start

An early burst of bubbles is usually Leuconostoc bacteria, not yeast. It fades as the mix turns acidic, and a quiet few days follow. This is normal: keep going and don't feed more. Wink 2008, Wild Yeast.

Whole grain and rye start faster

Flour is where the microbes come from, and whole grain and rye flours carry more of them, plus more minerals and sugars. In every side-by-side test, rye got going first. White flour starts slower and milder, but feeds a mature starter perfectly well. Celano 2016, Serious Eats, Sourdough Journey.

Warm, but not hot

Around 24 to 26 °C is the sweet spot for a new starter. Cooler kitchens work but take longer. Much above 27 °C favours the bacteria over the yeast and makes a more sour, less lively starter. Gänzle 1998, Minervini 2018, Wink 2008.

Feed small, then step up

While a starter is weak, big or frequent feeds dilute the acid it needs and slow it down. Once it rises reliably, larger feeds (1:2:2, then 1:5:5) give it more food, a milder flavour and a slower, more predictable peak. Minervini 2014, Wink 2008, Serious Eats.

Keep it the same

Same flour, same water, same routine. Each of these shapes which microbes settle in, and changing them resets part of the process. Your hands and kitchen add microbes too, which is why every starter turns out a little different. Reese 2020, Minervini 2019, Landis 2021.

Ready means repeatable

A starter is ready when it at least doubles within about 6 to 8 hours of a feed, several feeds in a row, and smells pleasantly sour. The calendar matters less than repeatable behaviour. The float test is unreliable, especially with rye and wetter starters. King Arthur, America's Test Kitchen.

Skip the tricks, know the warning signs

Raisins, grapes and commercial yeast don't help a starter. Grey liquid on top or a nail-polish smell just mean it is hungry. Pink or orange streaks or fuzzy mould mean start again. Modernist Cuisine, King Arthur.
